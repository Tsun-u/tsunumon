## Before You Begin: Know Your Student (CRITICAL for Adaptability scoring)

Read the `persona_analysis` in the outline above **before writing anything**. Every decision you make — slide complexity, vocabulary, analogies, examples, narration tone — must be tailored to THIS specific student:

1. **Who is the student?** Check `target_audience` for age, background, learning pace, and motivation.
2. **What do they already know?** Check `prior_knowledge` — this is your starting point. NEVER assume knowledge beyond this.
3. **What are their gaps?** Check `knowledge_gaps` — these are the concepts you must carefully scaffold, not dump.
4. **What format is this?** This is a **slide-based video lesson with TTS voiceover** — not a textbook, not a live lecture. Slides are glanced at briefly while the student listens. Keep slides visual and simple; put depth in the narration.

If the student is a high schooler, use high-school-appropriate language and relatable examples. If the student is an adult professional, use industry analogies. **Generic, one-size-fits-all content scores poorly on Adaptability.**

5. **How deep should you teach?** The essential knowledge MUST be fully covered — never skip required concepts. But **adjust your teaching METHOD based on the student's background**:
   - **Student WITH domain background** (e.g., physics student learning physics): Full mathematical derivations, formal notation on slides, step-by-step proofs — they can follow the rigor.
   - **Student WITHOUT domain background** (e.g., office worker learning physics): Teach the **spirit and intuition** instead of formal derivations. Use everyday analogies and visual imagery to convey the concept. Show formulas on slides as reference only — the narration should explain what the formula *means* in plain language, not walk through the algebra. Build the conceptual picture FIRST, then introduce symbols as labels for ideas they already understand.
   - Example: For projectile motion taught to a non-physics adult — instead of deriving `y = v₀sinθ·t - ½gt²`, say: "Imagine tossing a ball to a friend. Horizontally, it cruises at a steady speed. Vertically, gravity pulls it down harder and harder. These two motions happen independently — and THAT is the key insight."

### Slide Design Principles (IMPORTANT)
These are non-negotiable rules for good presentation design. Remember: slides will be displayed as a **video** — each slide appears for the duration of its narration segment. Students cannot pause, scroll, or re-read.

1. **ONE idea per slide.** If a topic has 3 sub-points, consider using 3 slides instead of cramming them into one.
2. **Minimum font size is 22px.** Never use font-size smaller than 22px for any text. Inline styles must respect this. The only exception is `<span class="page-num">`.
3. **Maximum 3-4 bullet points per slide.** If you have more points, split across multiple slides.
4. **Slides are visual aids, NOT documents.** Put details in the narration, not on the slide. The audience listens and glances at the slide — they don't read it.
5. **White space is good design.** Don't fill every pixel. A slide with 3 big words and lots of breathing room is better than a wall of text.
6. **Prefer splitting over shrinking.** When content is dense, create more slides with larger text. Never shrink text to fit.
7. **HIGH CONTRAST text is mandatory.** These slides will be projected in a classroom — every student in the back row must be able to read them clearly. Use dark text (#333 or darker) on light backgrounds. NEVER use white or light-colored text on pastel/light backgrounds. The only exception is `.code-block` (dark background with light text). When in doubt, make text darker.

For example, a "Summary" sequence with 3 takeaways + equations should become 3-4 slides:
- Slide A: "Revisiting our hook question" (answer the opening question)
- Slide B: "Key equations" (the formulas, big and clear)
- Slide C: "What you now know" (2-3 big takeaways)

### Audience-Facing Language Rules (MUST — applies to BOTH slide_html and narration_text)
The student is the audience. Our internal teaching-design vocabulary must NEVER leak into what they see or hear.

### Narration Guidelines
- **CRITICAL: `narration_text` is a voiceover script that will be read aloud by a Text-to-Speech engine.** It is NOT visual text — the audience will HEAR it, not read it. Therefore:
  - Write ALL mathematical expressions in spoken form: "v squared" not "v²", "x to the power of n" not "xⁿ", "delta x" not "Δx", "the integral of f of x" not "∫f(x)dx".
  - Write abbreviations and acronyms phonetically when they should be spelled out: "S-O-H C-A-H T-O-A" not "SOH CAH TOA", "A-T-P" not "ATP" (unless it's commonly pronounced as a word).
  - Avoid special characters, subscripts, superscripts, or Unicode symbols. Use plain English words instead.
  - Chemical formulas should be spoken: "C-O-2" or "carbon dioxide" not "CO₂", "H-2-O" or "water" not "H₂O".
  - Use words for symbols: "equals" not "=", "approximately" not "≈", "greater than" not ">".
- Write in natural, conversational English as if speaking to the student directly.
- Use "you" and "we" to create engagement: "Let's look at...", "Notice how..."
- Include verbal transitions between slides.
- Direct attention to visuals: "Take a look at...", "On the left side..."
- **Adaptability is KEY**: You already read the persona_analysis above — now apply it. Every explanation must start from what the student ALREADY knows and bridge to the new concept. Never introduce a term the student hasn't seen without first explaining it using concepts within their Zone of Proximal Development. Use analogies and examples drawn from the student's specific life experience (age, interests, background) — not generic textbook examples. If the student is 16, don't talk like a college professor. If they're a working professional, don't over-simplify.
- **Resonance — make the student feel "this is about ME"**: Don't just match their level — match their *world*. Use scenarios, language, and emotional hooks that resonate with the student's specific background. A high school athlete should hear sports analogies. A working professional should hear workplace scenarios. A student preparing for exams should feel the narration is coaching them to succeed. The goal is to make the student think "this teacher *gets* me" — not just "this teacher knows the subject."
- **Motivation — give the student a reason to care**: If the student persona lacks a clear learning motivation (e.g., "working professional" with no stated reason for learning this topic), you MUST create one in the hook. Connect the topic to something the student's life touches: health, technology, career, daily decisions, or curiosity. Example: for a professional learning cell biology — "Ever wondered why some drugs target cancer cells without harming healthy ones? The answer starts with how cells organize themselves." Without a compelling "why should I care?", Engagement scores will be low.
- Aim for ~150 words per minute of speech.
- Each segment's narration should match its estimated duration.
- **Worked examples**: Limit to 2-3 focused worked examples. Each example should serve a distinct purpose (e.g., basic application, then a twist, then reverse problem). Do NOT stack many similar examples — this causes fatigue, not mastery.
- **Extensions and applications**: Real-world connections and "what comes next" content should go in the final segments, not mid-lesson. They are value-add closers, not main content.
- **Keep students engaged and wanting to hear more:**
  - Vary sentence rhythm: alternate short punchy sentences with longer explanations. "That's it. Just two ATP. But here's the twist — those two little molecules are about to unlock a LOT more energy."
  - Use light humor and personality: make the occasional witty remark, fun analogy, or playful aside. "The mitochondria — yes, the powerhouse of the cell, you've heard that a million times — but let me tell you WHY it earned that title."
  - Create mini-cliffhangers between segments: "We've got glucose broken in half. But what happens next? That's where things get really clever."
  - Celebrate "aha moments": "See what just happened there? That one simple idea explains the entire process!"
  - Use contrast and surprise: "You might think this is complicated. It's actually beautifully simple."
  - React to the content emotionally: "This is honestly one of the coolest things in biology." Express genuine enthusiasm.
- **Teaching strategies** (use where appropriate):
  - Socratic questioning: "What do you think happens if...?" "Can you guess why...?"
  - Suspense/curiosity hooks: "Here's where it gets interesting..." "You might be surprised to learn..."
  - Mini-summaries at phase transitions: briefly recap before moving to the next big idea.
  - Vivid analogies and real-world examples to make abstract concepts tangible.
